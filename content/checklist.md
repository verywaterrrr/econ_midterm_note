# ECON2103 Midterm: Topics and Skills Checklist

**Scope:** Chapters 1–8, 14 and 15, based on the lecture slides and problem sets in the two Canvas folders. Your midterm is multiple choice, so the checklist emphasizes recognizing concepts, interpreting graphs, comparing statements and doing calculations. It is a study guide, not a confirmed list of examinable questions or weightings.

Use the boxes to track what you can explain and apply without looking at your notes. “Ch” refers to the course's chapter numbering. References below point to the original files in `Canvas/Lecture Slides/` and `Canvas/Problem Sets/`; slide numbers are the sequence in the PowerPoint.

## Chapter 1 — Ten Principles of Economics

**Sources:** Ch 1 slides, especially 6–34; `Chapter 1&2.pdf`, Ch 1 questions 2, 5, 7–8.

### Scarcity, choices and opportunity cost
- [ ] Explain scarcity, constraints and why choices involve trade-offs. Identify the alternatives forgone in a decision.
- [ ] Calculate the opportunity cost of an action, including money spent and the value of the **best** forgone alternative. Avoid adding the values of several mutually exclusive alternatives together.
- [ ] Compare alternatives using benefits and opportunity costs, including benefits that are not naturally expressed in dollars: ask what someone would be willing to pay for them.
- [ ] Recognize a sunk cost and exclude it from a forward-looking decision; compare the **additional** benefit and **additional** cost of proceeding.
- [ ] Apply marginal reasoning: take an additional action when its marginal benefit exceeds its marginal cost, and identify the relevant margin in a numerical question.
- [ ] Predict how a change in rewards, penalties or other incentives changes behavior.

### Interaction, markets and government
- [ ] Explain how specialization and voluntary trade can benefit participants.
- [ ] Define a market, market economy, property rights, and the “invisible hand”; explain how prices coordinate buyers' and sellers' choices.
- [ ] Distinguish efficiency from equality and identify the potential trade-off between them.
- [ ] Recognize externalities and market power as possible sources of market failure; identify whether an intervention is motivated by efficiency, equality or both.

**Slide scope note:** Slides 35–39 label the economy-as-a-whole principles “Not required*.” The Ch 1 problem set nevertheless contains a productivity question. Treat the slide label as a useful guide and check the instructor's exam instructions if you need certainty about that question.

## Chapter 2 — Thinking Like an Economist

**Sources:** Ch 2 slides 2–23, 29–44; `Chapter 1&2.pdf`, Ch 2 questions 2–4 and 6.

### Economic thinking and models
- [ ] Explain the role of assumptions, simplified models, observations and evidence in economic analysis.
- [ ] Distinguish microeconomics from macroeconomics, and positive statements that can be tested from normative judgments about what ought to happen.
- [ ] Explain why economists may disagree about evidence, model assumptions, parameter estimates or values.
- [ ] Read the circular-flow diagram: households own and sell factors of production and buy goods; firms buy factors and sell goods. Trace the corresponding flows of goods, inputs and money.

### Production possibilities frontier (PPF)
- [ ] Identify feasible, infeasible, efficient and inefficient points; explain why a point inside the frontier indicates unused or misallocated resources.
- [ ] Calculate the opportunity cost of one good in units of the other from a table or a PPF's slope. Interpret its magnitude and units.
- [ ] Explain a straight-line PPF with constant opportunity cost and a bowed-out PPF with increasing opportunity cost due to resources differing in suitability.
- [ ] Trace movements along the PPF versus shifts in the frontier. Show how a change in resources or good-specific technology affects production possibilities.
- [ ] Construct or interpret an economy-wide PPF from workers with different productivities, allocating production in order of comparative advantage; recognize inefficient allocations within the frontier.
- [ ] Compare the gains in one good from giving up a given quantity of another at different locations on a bowed frontier.

### Graphs and evidence
- [ ] Read axes, coordinates, intercepts and slope (`Δy/Δx`), including negative, zero and vertical slopes; distinguish a movement along a curve from a shift.
- [ ] Explain why correlation alone does not establish causation; recognize omitted variables and reverse causality in examples.

## Chapter 3 — Interdependence and the Gains from Trade

**Sources:** Ch 3 slides 2–23; `Chapter 3.pdf`, questions 3, 4, 6–8.

### Absolute and comparative advantage
- [ ] Determine absolute advantage from output per unit of input or inputs needed per unit of output.
- [ ] Calculate each producer's opportunity cost using either productivity data or time required. Express each answer in units of the other good, and use reciprocal costs consistently.
- [ ] Identify comparative advantage as the lower opportunity cost. Recognize that a producer can have absolute advantage in both goods and that trade can still create gains when opportunity costs differ.
- [ ] Determine each producer's efficient specialization and exports. Construct the combined PPF by assigning production to those with the lowest opportunity cost first.

### Gains and terms of trade
- [ ] Find a mutually beneficial range for the price of a good in terms of the other good: it must lie strictly between the two producers' opportunity costs for both to gain from that exchange.
- [ ] Use production and trade quantities to calculate consumption after trade, and compare each person's consumption with their no-trade possibilities.
- [ ] Recognize that trade can allow consumption beyond an individual producer's own PPF; aggregate production still uses available resources and technology.
- [ ] Analyze how a productivity change can alter comparative advantage and potential trade patterns. If both producers have identical opportunity-cost ratios, this simple two-good model has no comparative-advantage gain from specialization.

## Chapter 4 — The Market Forces of Supply and Demand

**Sources:** Ch 4 slides; `Chapter 4.pdf`, questions 3, 4, 7, 9–10.

### Demand and supply
- [ ] Define quantity demanded and quantity supplied; explain the laws of demand and supply, holding other influences constant.
- [ ] Distinguish a change in a good's own price, which moves along its curve, from a change in another determinant, which shifts the curve.
- [ ] Predict demand shifts caused by income (normal versus inferior goods), related goods (substitutes versus complements), tastes, expectations and the number of buyers.
- [ ] Predict supply shifts caused by input prices, production technology, expectations and the number of sellers.
- [ ] Add individual quantities horizontally at each price to obtain market demand or market supply.

### Equilibrium and changing markets
- [ ] Find equilibrium in a graph, schedule or equations by setting `Qd = Qs` and solving for price and quantity.
- [ ] Identify a shortage (`Qd > Qs`) or surplus (`Qs > Qd`) at a stated price and explain the usual pressure on price in an unrestricted market.
- [ ] Work out the direction of equilibrium price and quantity after one curve shifts. For simultaneous shifts, identify which outcome is definite and which depends on the relative shift sizes.
- [ ] Follow a chain across markets: for example, an input-price change affects a final good, which affects demand for a complement or substitute.
- [ ] Work backward from observed changes in price and quantity to determine which proposed demand or supply shift could explain them; identify when more than one explanation is possible.

## Chapter 5 — Elasticity and Its Application

**Sources:** Ch 5 slides; `Chapter 5.pdf`, questions 4–6, 9 and 12.

### Demand elasticity
- [ ] Calculate price elasticity of demand as the absolute percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. When comparing two points, use the midpoint formula: `|[(Q2−Q1)/((Q1+Q2)/2)] / [(P2−P1)/((P1+P2)/2)]|`.
- [ ] Classify demand as elastic (`> 1`), inelastic (`< 1`), unit elastic (`= 1`), perfectly inelastic (`= 0`) or perfectly elastic. Identify horizontal and vertical extreme cases.
- [ ] Explain how availability of substitutes, necessity versus luxury, market definition and time horizon influence elasticity.
- [ ] Distinguish elasticity from slope. On a linear demand curve, elasticity changes along the curve; a constant slope does not imply constant elasticity.
- [ ] Relate price changes to total revenue or expenditure `P × Q`: a price increase raises revenue when demand is inelastic and lowers it when demand is elastic; the reverse applies to a price decrease, holding the demand curve fixed.
- [ ] Interpret zero and unit elasticity in verbal situations, including fixed quantity purchased and fixed expenditure.
- [ ] Calculate and interpret income elasticity and cross-price elasticity; use their signs to classify normal/inferior goods and substitutes/complements.

### Supply elasticity and applications
- [ ] Calculate price elasticity of supply using percentage changes and explain why supply is often more elastic over a longer horizon.
- [ ] Apply elasticity to shifts: explain how the size of a price or quantity response depends on the elasticity of the curve being moved along.
- [ ] Analyze the revenue effects of a bumper crop versus a local crop failure, and supply restrictions in oil or illegal drugs; trace effects into related markets when needed.
- [ ] Evaluate proposed explanations for a price change with unchanged quantity, including perfectly inelastic curves and simultaneous shifts.

## Chapter 6 — Supply, Demand, and Government Policies

**Sources:** Ch 6 slides, including slide 28 on rationing; `Chapter 6&8.pdf`, Ch 6 questions 7, 8 and 10; `Self-Practice_Chapter 6.pdf`.

### Price ceilings and floors
- [ ] Identify a binding price ceiling below equilibrium and a binding price floor above equilibrium; recognize when a control has no effect and why.
- [ ] Calculate quantity demanded, quantity supplied, actual traded quantity, and the resulting shortage or surplus at a controlled price. With ordinary voluntary trading and no government purchase, the short side limits the amount traded.
- [ ] Explain non-price rationing, queues and potential quality changes under a binding ceiling; explain unsold supply under a binding floor.
- [ ] Apply a minimum wage to the labor market: workers supply labor, firms demand labor, employment is labor demanded at a binding wage floor, and unemployment is labor supplied minus labor demanded.
- [ ] Calculate total wage payments as wage times employment. Use the elasticity of labor demand to assess how a wage increase affects total payments; use both labor supply and demand responses to assess unemployment.
- [ ] Compare short- and long-run shortages when supply and demand become more elastic, as in rent control.
- [ ] Assess consumer and producer surplus under controls carefully: the identity of buyers or sellers who actually trade matters. Without an efficient-rationing assumption, the exact welfare areas may not be determined from supply and demand curves alone.

### Unit taxes and incidence
- [ ] Draw or interpret a tax on sellers as an upward supply shift, and a tax on buyers as a downward demand shift, by the tax per unit.
- [ ] Solve for the buyer's price `Pb`, seller's net price `Ps`, and quantity using `Pb − Ps = tax`, `Qd(Pb) = Qs(Ps)`.
- [ ] Calculate the buyer's per-unit burden `Pb − P0` and seller's per-unit burden `P0 − Ps`, where `P0` is the original price; the burdens sum to the tax.
- [ ] Explain why economic incidence depends on relative elasticities rather than the side legally required to remit the tax: the less elastic side generally bears more.
- [ ] Apply the same reasoning to payroll taxes and identify employer labor cost versus the wage received by workers.

## Chapter 7 — Consumers, Producers, and the Efficiency of Markets

**Sources:** Ch 7 slides 2–30 and 38–41; `Chapter 7.pdf`, questions 4–6, 10–11.

### Consumer and producer surplus
- [ ] Interpret willingness to pay, the demand curve and the marginal buyer. Compute consumer surplus as willingness to pay minus the price paid, summed over units purchased.
- [ ] Interpret sellers' opportunity costs, the supply curve and the marginal seller. Compute producer surplus as the price received minus cost, summed over units sold.
- [ ] Identify consumer surplus as the area below demand and above the buyer's price, and producer surplus as the area above supply and below the seller's price, over the units actually traded.
- [ ] Use rectangles, triangles or discrete unit values to calculate and compare surplus as prices change. Separate gains to existing participants from gains to new participants.
- [ ] Compare a fixed-fee service with a per-use charge: identify the marginal cost faced by the consumer, the quantity chosen and net benefit after the fixed fee.

### Efficiency and welfare
- [ ] Calculate total surplus as consumer surplus plus producer surplus, or buyers' total value minus sellers' total cost in an untaxed market.
- [ ] Explain why, in the competitive model without relevant market failures, the equilibrium quantity maximizes total surplus: trade units for which marginal value is at least marginal cost.
- [ ] Identify lost gains from trade when quantity is below the efficient level, and the loss from units whose marginal cost exceeds marginal value when quantity is above it.
- [ ] Distinguish efficiency from equality and identify circumstances, including market power and externalities, where the simple competitive-efficiency conclusion may not hold.
- [ ] Apply marginal analysis to insurance: compare the consumer's out-of-pocket price with society's marginal cost, and explain the possible effect on the quantity of care.

## Chapter 8 — Application: The Costs of Taxation

**Sources:** Ch 8 slides, especially 2–15, 21–24 and 30–32; `Chapter 6&8.pdf`, Ch 8 questions 7, 8 and 10; `Self-Practice_Chapter 8.pdf`.

### Tax revenue and deadweight loss
- [ ] On a supply-and-demand graph, identify the pretax equilibrium, the tax wedge, the prices paid and received, and the quantity traded after the tax.
- [ ] Calculate government revenue as `tax per unit × quantity traded after tax`; distinguish buyer spending `Pb × Q` from seller receipts `Ps × Q`.
- [ ] Compute consumer surplus, producer surplus and government revenue after a tax. In the standard model, total surplus is `CS + PS + tax revenue`.
- [ ] Identify deadweight loss (DWL) as the lost gains from trades no longer made. For linear curves and an interior equilibrium, calculate `DWL = ½ × tax per unit × (Q before − Q after)`.
- [ ] Explain that a tax may reduce consumer and producer surplus by more than the government collects; the difference is DWL.
- [ ] Compare a tax's effects when demand or supply is more elastic, holding the tax and relevant initial conditions fixed. A larger quantity response creates a larger DWL.
- [ ] Recognize that a tax on a perfectly inelastic side can raise revenue with zero DWL in this model; understand that zero revenue does not by itself establish zero DWL.
- [ ] Assess whether buyer spending or seller revenue rises or falls after a tax using the demand and supply conditions; a lower seller price and lower quantity reduce seller receipts for ordinary positive prices.

### Tax size, time and subsidies
- [ ] Trace how an increasing unit tax affects quantity, revenue and DWL; interpret the Laffer curve and recognize that tax revenue need not always increase when the tax rate rises.
- [ ] Compare short- and long-run effects when elasticities change. The optional elasticity/Laffer discussion is identified on slide 24.
- [ ] Analyze a per-unit subsidy: `Ps − Pb = subsidy`; quantity typically rises, buyers pay less, sellers receive more, and government expenditure is subsidy times quantity.
- [ ] Calculate subsidy welfare as `CS + PS − government spending`; identify the potential DWL from units whose production cost exceeds buyers' valuation in the standard competitive model.
- [ ] Compare a subsidy and a binding price ceiling in terms of price, quantity, shortage and government spending; allow for market failures or other stated assumptions when evaluating policy.

## Chapter 14 — The Costs of Production

**Sources:** Ch 14 slides 2–30; `Chapter 14.pdf`, questions 4 and 7–9; `Self-Practice_Chapter 14.pdf`.

### Costs, revenue and profit
- [ ] Calculate total revenue `TR = P × Q`; distinguish explicit costs from implicit opportunity costs, including forgone wages and interest on the owner's funds.
- [ ] Calculate accounting profit as revenue minus explicit costs and economic profit as revenue minus both explicit and implicit costs. Interpret zero economic profit as covering all opportunity costs.
- [ ] Apply marginal decisions using the change in **total** cost, including when average cost changes between output levels; average cost itself is not the cost of one extra unit.

### Production and cost curves
- [ ] Calculate marginal product `MP = Δoutput / Δinput` from a production table; recognize increasing and diminishing marginal product and explain the shape of the production function.
- [ ] Explain, with a fixed input price and other conditions held constant, why diminishing marginal product tends to raise marginal cost. Relate the shape of the production function to the total-cost curve.
- [ ] Identify fixed cost `FC`, variable cost `VC`, and total cost `TC = FC + VC`; recover any missing value in a cost table.
- [ ] Calculate `AFC = FC/Q`, `AVC = VC/Q`, `ATC = TC/Q = AFC + AVC`, and `MC = ΔTC/ΔQ = ΔVC/ΔQ` when fixed cost does not change. Average costs require `Q > 0`.
- [ ] Read and draw the typical curves: AFC falls with output; MC crosses AVC and ATC at their minima; MC below an average pulls it down and MC above it pulls it up.
- [ ] Identify efficient scale as the output minimizing ATC; calculate it from a cost schedule or graph.
- [ ] Predict which cost curves change with a fixed lump-sum tax versus a per-unit production tax: the former changes FC, AFC and ATC; the latter changes VC, AVC, ATC and MC.

### Short run and long run
- [ ] Distinguish fixed inputs in the short run from the ability to vary all inputs and plant scale in the long run.
- [ ] Identify economies of scale, constant returns to scale and diseconomies of scale from how long-run average total cost changes with output; explain specialization and coordination as possible reasons.

## Chapter 15 — Firms in Competitive Markets

**Sources:** Ch 15 slides 2–38; `Chapter 15.pdf`, questions 1, 4, 5, 10–11.

### Competitive firm and output choice
- [ ] Identify a competitive market's assumptions: many buyers and sellers, identical products and price-taking behavior; understand free entry and exit as the long-run model assumption.
- [ ] Calculate `TR = P × Q`, `AR = TR/Q`, and `MR = ΔTR/ΔQ`. For a price-taking firm, `P = AR = MR`.
- [ ] Find the profit-maximizing output by comparing total profit across feasible quantities or producing up to the point where marginal revenue equals marginal cost on the relevant rising MC segment. With discrete units, compare the extra revenue and extra cost of each unit.
- [ ] Check the shutdown choice as well as candidate outputs: an `MR = MC` intersection alone does not establish the global profit maximum, especially with multiple intersections or discrete output.
- [ ] Calculate profit or loss as `TR − TC = (P − ATC) × Q`; identify the profit or loss rectangle on a graph.

### Shutdown, exit and supply
- [ ] Apply the short-run shutdown rule: shut down when `P < AVC` at the best operating quantity, or equivalently `TR < VC`; fixed costs remain payable. At equality, the firm is indifferent under the model.
- [ ] Distinguish operating at a loss from shutting down: a firm may continue if revenue covers variable costs and contributes toward fixed costs.
- [ ] Apply the long-run exit rule `P < ATC`, and entry incentive when economic profit is positive; at equality, the firm earns zero economic profit.
- [ ] Identify the firm's short-run supply as the relevant rising part of MC at or above AVC, with zero output below the shutdown threshold; identify its long-run supply relative to ATC.
- [ ] Sum individual quantities supplied horizontally to find market supply with a fixed number of firms. Given market demand and the number of identical firms, solve for the short-run market price, market quantity, firm output and profit.

### Long-run equilibrium and changes
- [ ] Trace how positive profit induces entry and shifts market supply right, while losses induce exit and shift supply left, under free entry and exit.
- [ ] In the standard identical-firm, free-entry long-run equilibrium, identify `P = MR = MC = minimum ATC`, zero economic profit, and production at efficient scale. Determine market quantity and number of firms from demand and output per firm.
- [ ] Explain how a demand change affects price and profits in the short run, then entry or exit and the long-run outcome. Understand why long-run industry supply can be horizontal under identical constant-cost conditions and upward sloping if input costs rise or firms differ.
- [ ] Analyze a change in an input price or a firm's technology: shift the appropriate cost curves, determine short-run profits, and trace potential long-run industry adjustment. Consider whether a patented innovation can be copied when assessing entry or competition.

## Cross-chapter multiple-choice checks

- [ ] Label units and the relevant price: market price, buyer price, seller's net price, wage received or employer cost.
- [ ] Check whether a question asks for a **change in a curve** or a **movement along** it, and whether other determinants are held constant.
- [ ] Distinguish total, average and marginal values; use the marginal value for an additional-unit decision.
- [ ] When a numerical answer depends on a graph, identify the quantity actually traded and any stated assumptions about rationing, taxes or subsidies before calculating areas.
- [ ] For a before-and-after question, distinguish a participant's gain or loss from the change in total surplus and from government revenue or expenditure.
